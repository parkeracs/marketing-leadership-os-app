> [!summary] Principles
> - Five failure modes: inadequate revenue performance, lack of market/product/customer insight, eroding trust with the exec team, not being trusted to scale, and failing to build the right leadership team.
> - Trust buys leeway. A CMO with strained C-suite relationships (CEO, CFO, CRO, CPO) gets no time to turn lagging numbers around; one with trust and visible change often does.
> - Missed expectations start when the expectations are set. Use deep bottom-up analysis to negotiate KPIs/OKRs with the CEO and board, and don't sign up for an unattainable number.

> [!warning] Numbers and tactics ⏳
> - CMOs turn over faster than other C-suite roles, often within 2–3 years (as of 2024-02)
> - The most common board question to marketing: "If I gave you $XM, where would you put it, and how much new revenue?" (as of 2024-02)
> - Marketing patterns change sharply at stage gates of roughly $15M, $50M, $150M, $300M and $1B (as of 2024-02)

## Notes
- Marketing often gets fired before sales or finance for the same revenue miss, because its contribution (influence, awareness) is less directly measurable. The answer is a revenue dashboard and weekly report integrated with sales, with no ambiguity on what's measured.
- Performance problems are sometimes unfair: a lack of product-market fit, weak differentiation or poor sales conversion surfaces first in marketing, where the idea hits the market.
- CEOs often hire a demand-gen step-up candidate, then get frustrated that the CMO can't spar with them on company, product and market strategy. Invest in product marketing and customer time.
- A CEO judges the leadership team by whether it looks like the team that will drive growth two years out. Junior directs or untouched weak players make the CMO look stretched thin.
- Advisors can help a CMO who grew within the role learn the next stage's patterns and earn the CEO's confidence to scale.
- Being fired doesn't define a career; most exceptional CMOs have been fired.
