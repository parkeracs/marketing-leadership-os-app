> [!summary] Principles
> - Positioning is context-setting, like a film's opening scene: the frame you choose triggers buyer assumptions about competitors, features, buyer and price. Good positioning triggers true assumptions; bad positioning leaves sales to undo false ones.
> - Five components, worked in a fixed order: competitive alternatives → differentiated capabilities → value → best-fit customers → market category. Each depends on the one before; starting from alternatives is what keeps the value differentiated.
> - Skip the fill-in-the-blanks positioning statement. It assumes one right answer already exists in your head, when most products could credibly sit in several categories.
> - Competitive alternatives are what the customer would do if you didn't exist, including the status quo. Drop 'phantom competitors' you never meet in deals.
> - You rarely need a new category to grow. Most winners started as a niche of an existing category and stretched it later, once they had money and momentum.

> [!warning] Numbers and tactics ⏳ as of 2021-03
> - April Dunford (positioning expert, author of *Obviously Awesome*) put enterprise software's loss rate to "no decision" at about 25% of deals in 2021. Her later figures run at 40–60%, so use the newer ones (see the 2023 and 2026 notes).
> - April Dunford claims that 90% of the tech companies that went public in the five years before 2021 were positioned in existing markets, an argument against rushing to create a new category (source not given).
> - April Dunford's example: Janna Systems repositioned from "Enterprise CRM" to "CRM for investment banks" and grew from under $2M to close to $80M in revenue in about 18 months (claimed).
> - April Dunford's planned path for a niche position: start with a niche category (CRM for investment banks), then broaden it once you have momentum (CRM for financial services).
