> [!summary] Principles
> - Finance builds top-down; marketing must build a detailed bottom-up model to test it. The CEO and CFO won't move a target because it "feels" too hard, but they will move it on sound calculations.
> - Sequence: C-suite sets strategy and big levers, then finance makes an initial plan, then a marketing team strategy session on bringing the corporate strategy to life, then bottom-up modelling.
> - Close the top-down/bottom-up gap with explicit, shared assumptions (including buffers, or even a named "lucky bump"), never silently. Buffers too far from reality kill discipline around the stated goal.

> [!warning] Numbers and tactics ⏳
> - Internal goals often run 5–15% above the board/street goal as a buffer (as of 2024-10)
> - At Atlassian in hypergrowth, 75–85% of pipeline was organic inbound; organic leads can convert 2–3x better than some paid channels (as of 2024-10)
> - Recommends Uptempo's 7-Step Blueprint for Marketing Planning as a structured outline (vendor content) (as of 2024-10)

## Notes
- Four bottom-up cuts, often built in parallel and reconciled around one number: by channel, by product, by sales region or partner, by campaign.
- Gotcha 1: resisting the urge to simplify. Blending segments, regions or months hides seasonality and recency trends; the detail paid off.
- Gotcha 2: a budget model (e.g. CAC ratios) isn't a pipeline model. Model organic and paid separately; blended conversion rates break when the channel mix shifts.
- A detailed plan becomes next year's activity structure and reporting, so you execute against it rather than build as you go. That requires trustworthy current-year data first.
- The "lucky bump" was modelled with the CEO as an explicit joint assumption to close a 10–15% gap. It worked once; she doesn't endorse it as best practice, especially in tough markets.
