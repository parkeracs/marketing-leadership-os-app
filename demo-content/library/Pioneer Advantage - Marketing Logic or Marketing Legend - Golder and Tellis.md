> [!summary] Principles
> - **Earlier "first-mover advantage" studies were survivor-biased.** They relied on surveys of companies still alive, so failed pioneers vanished from the data. Using historical records instead reverses the conclusion.
> - **Being first is not what wins; committing resources to build the market is.** Early leaders typically spotted the opportunity and invested heavily once the market started to grow. The growth phase often coincides with their market-building, not the pioneer's.
> - **Continuous innovation inside the category beats being first** (Gillette cannibalising its own razors to stay ahead).
> - **A deliberate option is to let someone else pioneer and enter as the market forms.** This is the strategic case for fast-follower positioning in an emerging category.
> - **Caveat from the authors:** the study doesn't model the marketing mix or management quality, and past patterns don't guarantee future ones.

> [!warning] Numbers and tactics ⏳ as of 1993-05
> - Golder and Tellis's peer-reviewed study (Journal of Marketing Research) used historical records on about 500 brands across 50 consumer and industrial product categories, not software.
> - They found 47% of market pioneers failed and only 11% were still market leaders, and surviving pioneers' average market share was far lower than earlier survivor-biased studies claimed.
> - Early market leaders entered on average about 13 years after the pioneer (about 5 years in post-Second World War categories) and outperformed pioneers on share, survival and leadership. Use it for the mechanism, not as a software benchmark.
