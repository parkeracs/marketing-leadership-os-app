> [!summary] Principles
> - **Proactively assert what to do.** Don't ask "what should I do?"; bring a recommendation with rationale, the risk and its mitigation, and the first step if approved. It takes on the leader's decision load and gives them something concrete to push back on.
> - **Information hierarchy and the punchline first**: the ask or recommendation at the top, context below, and be explicit about what kind of response you need and by when.
> - **Trust loop**: say what you'll do, do it, say you're doing it, say you did it. Bad news goes up at the first itch, so your manager never hears it first from their own boss.

> [!warning] Numbers and tactics ⏳
> - Message templates such as "I recommend X. Here's why. Risk is Y, mitigated by Z. If you agree, I'll start with [step]" (as of 2023-10)
> - Borrows Andy Grove's "task-relevant maturity" to set how often to update: more updates on unfamiliar work, fewer once trust is earned (as of 2023-10)

## Notes
- Long-form compendium; the 15 cover embracing managing up, punchline first, showing thought process, flagging issues, solutions not complaints, keeping your manager in the loop, over-communication, sharing a point of view, anticipating questions, being explicit about needs, and expecting to manage up forever.
- Core empathy point: your area is all of your time but a small slice of your manager's, and they carry the accountability for your output.
- "Anticipate questions": the fastest yeses come when evidence answers the obvious objection before it's asked.
- Show your level of conviction; half-formed views are fine if labelled as such.
- Explicit limit: none of this works with a manager acting in bad faith ("know when to get out").
