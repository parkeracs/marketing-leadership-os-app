> [!summary] Principles
> - The pitch has two unequal parts. A short setup about the market (insight, the pluses and minuses of the alternatives, an agreed 'perfect world') then the follow-through about you (intro, each value theme with the capabilities that deliver it, proof, unspoken objections, the ask).
> - Teach the buyer how to buy. Most buyers have never bought software like yours and need to justify the choice to their boss; failing to help them is how deals die in 'no decision'.
> - Don't use FOMO on indecisive buyers. Matt Dixon's JOLT research found extra pressure lowers close rates. De-risk the decision instead (smaller commitments, guarantees, services, champion materials).
> - Roll out by piloting: train your best rep, test on qualified new prospects (not polluted existing customers), huddle after each call, and when the rep won't go back to the old pitch, have them sell it to the rest of sales.
> - Marketing ends up as steward ('positioning police') of positioning and pitch. Somebody must own it, often PMM, with quarterly rep recertification.

> [!warning] Numbers and tactics ⏳ as of 2023-10
> - April Dunford (positioning expert, author of *Obviously Awesome* and *Sales Pitch*) says 40–60% of B2B purchase processes end in no decision, which is why a pitch has to teach the buyer how to buy.
> - April Dunford's timeline for a new pitch: the positioning workshop takes a week, and a new pitch can be live in two weeks, though most companies take about a month.
> - April Dunford reports that some of her clients doubled first-call-to-opportunity conversion by tightening the sales pitch, and that in-house she saw revenue double within two quarters after a big repositioning (claimed).
> - According to April Dunford, category creators such as Salesforce, Qualtrics and Snowflake mostly start as a niche of an existing category and only stretch the category once they reach $200–400M in revenue.
