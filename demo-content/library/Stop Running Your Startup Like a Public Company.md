> [!summary] Principles
> - **Treat the annual plan as a hypothesis, not a contract.** Still build it, align the team and hold people accountable, but manage a rolling four-quarter plan updated with the latest quarter's actuals.
> - **The typical failure:** a detailed bottom-up plan in Q4, a miss in Q1, more hiring to catch up, then falling further behind. Misses are usually a system problem (demand generation, conversion or customer value), not a lack of effort or headcount. Hiring into a broken system deepens the hole.
> - **"Stay-or-Go-or-Slow":** at the start of each quarter, while emotions are low, the exec team and board pre-agree what would justify going faster, staying the course or slowing down. Review at quarter-end, re-plan four quarters out and reset the criteria.
> - **Criteria cover three systems:** demand generation (enough quality opportunities?), demand conversion (reps converting at expected rates and cycle times?) and customer value (customers getting value, so set up to renew and expand?). All green, go. None red, stay. Any red, slow, fix it, then re-accelerate without waiting for next quarter.
> - **It changes the board conversation** from defending misses to diagnosing root causes.

## Notes
- Written from the investor/CEO seat for startups below true scale. At a large or public company the annual plan carries more weight, as Roberge acknowledges.
- Marketing's pipeline is one of the three gates on the company's growth pace. A CMO who co-writes the demand-generation criteria with the CRO has a hand in how the board judges the whole GTM system, not just marketing.
- Fits with Kellogg's "make a plan you can beat" and "have the ambition argument now". Roberge adds a mechanism for revising the plan without reopening the fight every quarter.
