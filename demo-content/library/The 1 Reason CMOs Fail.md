> [!summary] Principles
> - By the time a company hires a CMO, the CEO already has a tested view of what works in marketing; the #1 failure mode is the CMO substituting their own scaling vision for it.
> - The CMO's job is to execute the CEO's vision for how the market sees the company, and to scale what already works: build on the current engine, align with the CEO's view, add cheap experiments.
> - Before the year starts, walk through everything marketing does with the CEO and agree the next 12 months' priorities. Unresolved misalignment compounds as budget and headcount grow, unless revenue shows up almost at once.

> [!warning] Numbers and tactics ⏳
> - A CMO (vs a VP Demand Gen / VP Marketing) becomes necessary at roughly $20M ARR, or 12–24 months after core marketing functions are built and working (as of 2022-12)
> - Most CEOs hold strong marketing opinions by about $10M ARR (as of 2022-12)

## Notes
- A CMO is a "quarterback" across demand gen, field, customer, product, brand, events, AR, growth and PR. They are hired once demand gen is routine and a brand exists, not as a demand-gen specialist.
- CEO preferences are concrete and channel-level (likes events and press, hates webinars; loves SMB growth, won't fund enterprise lead gen). Find them out explicitly rather than inferring them.
- Diverging from the CEO's strategic direction is a bet you must win, and in marketing being fully right is rare. The bar for a contrarian plan is high.
- Lemkin also tells CEOs not to expect magic: the right expectation is someone who makes the existing engine scale through the next phases.
- The page embeds a 2026 Lemkin post with the same line: object respectfully, do it your way where you can, then fall in line. Blunt CEO-side view; it underweights the cases where the CEO's instincts are the problem (see [[Carilu Dietrich]] on making plans change through modelling).
- Fails when: the CEO has never been the company's public face or marketer. Lemkin concedes the CMO then has more room.
