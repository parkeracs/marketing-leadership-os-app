> [!summary] Principles
> - **CMO turnover is mostly a role-design problem, not a talent problem.** When responsibilities, expectations and performance measures don't line up, the role sets any occupant up to fail.
> - **There are three CMO roles, and companies rarely say which they're hiring:** strategist (positioning and product decisions), commercialiser (drives sales through marketing communications) and enterprise P&L leader (both). CEOs often pick the candidate they like most instead of first defining the role.
> - **Expectations routinely exceed authority,** made worse when a CEO oversells the job to woo a candidate. A CMO judged on firm growth but owning only communications is in an impossible seat.
> - **Leaders blame the person, not the design.** The 2025 follow-up argues the shift from an ownership role to an influence role raises risk: the CMO spends time persuading peers who hold the decision rights.
> - **For a new CMO:** surface the role type and the measures in the first conversations with the CEO, before results are judged against a job that was never written down.

> [!warning] Numbers and tactics ⏳ as of 2017-07
> - Kimberly Whitler and Neil Morgan's Harvard Business Review survey of CMOs found over 40% had been in role two years or less, and 57% three years or less.
> - Of 170 CMO job descriptions Whitler and Morgan reviewed, only 22% said how the CMO would be measured and 2% spelt out expectations. Evidence that poorly defined roles drive CMO turnover.
> - Whitler and Morgan's survey found 31% of CMOs were strategists (positioning and product decisions), 46% commercialisers (driving sales through marketing communications) and 23% enterprise P&L leaders (both).
> - A 2025 follow-up study in the Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science found 54% of CMO roles misaligned, and 45% of CMOs had experience that didn't fit their role's scope (as of 2025-05).
