> [!summary] Principles
> - **Context before numbers.** Boards need the strategy behind the plan, the proposed org design (and the alternatives considered), and a short list of goals (5–7 OKRs at most) linked to the strategy. Without it, management shows up with a budget, not a plan.
> - **Give financial context:** one year of history, the current-year forecast, the proposed plan, and the two out-years the plan puts you on. Showing the out-years blocks the oldest trick, hitting this year's burn by underinvesting in H2 for next year's growth, and builds board consensus on trajectory.
> - **Present scenarios side by side.** In software, scenarios are usually versions of the plan optimised for different things: **baseline** (management's best balance), **growth** (optimises growth at the expense of cash or CAC) and **profit** (optimises runway). Show each one's plan-year and next-year model, with a handful of key rows.
> - **Know what two slides miss:** segment analysis when the business blends motions, a section on any strategic challenge the plan depends on (e.g. hitting a cost per opportunity), and evidence of zero-based budgeting in tight years.

> [!warning] Numbers and tactics ⏳ as of 2022-11
> - Dave Kellogg (enterprise software executive and board member) says that in a SaaS operating plan, expansion ARR at about 10% of new ARR suggests the company is neglecting existing customers, while 40% raises questions about new-logo acquisition unless it runs a land-and-expand model. A quick sense-check on the new-versus-expansion mix before the plan reaches the board.
> - Kellogg's two core slides for presenting an operating plan to the board: first, the ARR "leaky bucket" with headcount of quota-carrying reps, CSMs and developers and their productivity per head; second, an abbreviated P&L showing CAC ratio, CAC payback, Rule of 40 and ending cash.
