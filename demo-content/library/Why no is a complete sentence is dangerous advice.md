> [!summary] Principles
> - How much explanation a "no" needs depends on two variables: **the power dynamic** with the asker and **the legitimacy of the business request**. A peer exec or the CEO with a legitimate ask is owed a reason.
> - A bare "no" to a legitimate request costs you reciprocity later; the aim is not niceness but keeping counterparts willing to help you.
> - Turn yes/no into a prioritisation conversation: name the trade-off and hand the asker the choice, so you get the outcome of a no without being "not a team player".

> [!warning] Numbers and tactics ⏳
> - Five moves (as of 2025-09): (1) "I can't do that, but I can do this", ending on the affirmative; (2) cite trade-offs: "I can, but it delays X; which do you want first?"; (3) ask what it's for before deciding; (4) add a "because" tied to a business priority; (5) give the benefit of the doubt to well-meaning colleagues
> - Cites a 1970s Harvard study that "because" increases compliance regardless of the reason (as of 2025-09)

## Notes
- Argues against the boundary-setting slogan for workplace use; the reasons needed take seconds, not minutes.
- Asking about the purpose often reveals a cheaper alternative that serves the asker better, or shows the request genuinely should jump the queue.
- Trade-off framing works best when the asker is also the one who set your other priorities (your boss, or a peer whose earlier requests are in flight).
- "Check with my manager given current priorities" is offered as a legitimate deferral.
- Fails with people who are overstepping in bad faith; she says to drop the benefit of the doubt there.
