> [!summary] Principles
> - "Make a plan that you can beat" (advice he credits to Mike Moritz). "Can beat" means neither a certainty nor a miracle: realistic and aggressive at once.
> - Have the ambition argument with the board during planning, while you're still delivering this year, not mid-year after missing two quarters.
> - Build the bookings/revenue plan first and derive expenses from it. Never get "talked up" on revenue to protect an expense number you've anchored on. If it won't close, argue the profit or cash goal instead.

> [!warning] Numbers and tactics ⏳
> - Example of a gated bonus plan: nothing below a threshold share of plan, a partial payout at the gate, linear to full payout at plan, accelerators above it (as of 2014-11)
> - His track record cited: hit plan in most quarters over six years at MarkLogic (as of 2014-11)

## Notes
- Argues a beatable plan keeps you employed, protects cash, shows you're in control, fits how exec comp is designed, and leaves room to speed up later. Boards readily approve upward revisions when leading indicators are strong.
- Treat the plan's growth rate as the number you're willing to be fired for missing, not the one you aspire to.
- Separates this from sandbagging. Persistent under-forecasting is harmful. Persistent overachievement is either great execution or skilled target negotiation, and market-share movement tells you which.
- Performance should be normalised to the market. Missing an over-ambitious target while gaining share is a planning failure, not an execution failure.
- Written from the CEO's seat. A CMO uses it by holding the line against pipeline targets inflated to make an expense envelope work.
